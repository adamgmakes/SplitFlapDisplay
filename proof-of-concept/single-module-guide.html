SPLITFLAP

Single-module split-flap display — beginner build guide

You've printed and assembled the module — this guide covers everything electrical: soldering four wires, flashing the chip, wiring it to a Raspberry Pi, and making the drum spin to any letter you type. Every term is explained as it appears, and nothing here involves mains voltage: the whole project runs at 12 volts, which is safe to touch.

01 How your one-module system fits together

Four things talk to each other. A Raspberry Pi runs a small Python program and speaks ordinary serial from two pins on its GPIO header. A small converter module turns that into RS-485, a two-wire signal designed to travel along noisy cables. The driver board on the back of your module listens on those two wires and drives the motor to rotate the drum. A wall adapter provides 12 V to power the motor.

RS-485 is just a hardier version of a serial cable — two data wires, called A and B, shared by every module. Each message starts with a module number, so in the full display all 45 modules hear everything but only one acts. Your proof of concept is the same bus with one listener — and the Pi drives it the same way in the finished display, so none of this wiring is throwaway.

04 Solder four wires into the pogo-pin holes

≈ 15 minutes · the only soldering in this project

Cut four lengths of solid-core wire, roughly 30 cm each — long enough to reach your desk comfortably. Use the colour convention above (red = 12V, black = G, blue = A, green = B); every later step assumes it.

  1. Strip about 3 mm of insulation from one end of each wire.
  2. Push the bare end into its hole from the back of the board so the wire exits away from the module's mechanism.
  3. Touch the iron to the wire and the ring around the hole together for a second, then feed in a little solder. A good joint looks like a small shiny cone, not a ball.
  4. Tug each wire gently — it shouldn't move.

Check before moving on: the red wire must be in the hole marked 12V and black in G. Swapping power and ground is the one mistake in this project that can destroy the board. Getting A and B backwards, by contrast, is harmless — the display just stays silent until you swap them.

Strip about 6 mm from the free ends — that's what clamps into screw terminals later. While the iron is hot, also solder the 3-pin female header into the board's UPDI Interface holes — it makes the next step plug-and-play.

05 Flash the firmware

≈ 30 minutes, mostly one-time software setup

Flashing means copying the program (firmware) onto the board's chip, an ATtiny1616. This chip has no USB socket, so the UPDI Friend acts as the bridge: USB on the computer side, three wires on the chip side. UPDI is just the name of the protocol those three wires speak. You'll do this once — settings survive power-off.

5a — One-time Arduino setup

  1. Install the Arduino IDE (free).
  2. Open Settings → Additional boards manager URLs and paste: 
    http://drazzy.com/package_drazzy.com_index.json
  3. Open Tools → Board → Boards Manager, search megaTinyCore, install it. (This teaches Arduino about the ATtiny1616.)
  4. Under Tools, set:
    • Board → megaTinyCore → ATtiny3216/1616/1606/816/806/416/406
    • Chip → ATtiny1616
    • Programmer → SerialUPDI — 230400 baud
    • Save EEPROM → retained (keeps settings through future reflashes)
    • Leave clock and everything else at defaults.

5b — Set the module's address

Open firmware/splitflapfirmwarev7/splitflapfirmwarev7.ino in the IDE. Near line 40, find and change: 

const uint8_t HARDCODED_ID = 38;   //  change 38 → 0

Every module on a bus needs a unique number; yours will be module 0. (In the eventual full display, 0 is the top-left position.)

5c — Connect the UPDI Friend and upload

  1. Set the UPDI Friend's voltage switch to 3V.
  2. Plug its cable's three pins into the board's header: red → +3V3, black → G, white → UPDI.
  3. Plug the Friend into the computer over USB-C. Its power light should come on.
  4. In the IDE: Tools → Port → the entry like /dev/cu.usbserial-….
  5. Choose Sketch → Upload Using Programmer (⌘⇧U — not the normal Upload button).
  6. Wait for "Done uploading", then unplug the Friend. The board's chip now remembers it is module 0, forever.

06 Wire everything together

≈ 10 minutes · screwdriver only, no soldering

  1. Push four female-female jumpers onto the Pi's GPIO header and the converter's pins: pin 1 (3V3) → VCC, pin 6 (GND) → GND, pin 8 (TXD) → RXD, pin 10 (RXD) → TXD.
  2. Screw the blue wire into the converter terminal marked A+, and green into B−.
  3. Screw the red wire into the + terminal of the barrel adapter and the black into −. Most screw adapters print + and − on the housing; double-check yours.
  4. Leave the 12 V adapter unplugged from the wall until step 08.

Nothing here bites. 12 V at these currents can't hurt you — the worst realistic outcome of a mistake is a dead board (only from swapping + and −, which you already double-checked). The mains side stays sealed inside the wall adapter, and the converter's isolation protects the Pi from any wiring mishap on the bus side.

07 Enable the Pi's serial port

≈ 5 minutes · one-time

The Pi's GPIO serial pins are normally reserved for a login console. Hand them over to your display: 

sudo raspi-config
# Interface Options → Serial Port →
#   "Would you like a login shell over serial?"      → No
#   "Would you like the serial port hardware enabled?" → Yes
sudo reboot

After the reboot the port exists as /dev/serial0 — that name works on every Pi model.

08 First power-on

≈ 2 minutes · the moment of truth

  1. Final glance: red in 12V, black in G, motor and Hall sensor plugged in, drum free to spin.
  2. Plug the 12 V adapter into the wall.
  3. What you should see: almost immediately (module 0 has no start-up delay), the drum rotates — possibly a full revolution — then stops precisely at the blank flap. That's the module homing: spinning until the magnet in the drum passes the Hall sensor, then counting a known number of steps to flap zero.

If it did that: the electronics all work. If instead the drum spins for a while and gives up, or nothing moves at all, unplug and jump to the troubleshooting table — both symptoms have short fix lists.

09 Drive it from the Pi

≈ 10 minutes

The repo ships an interactive test tool. SSH into the Pi (or open a terminal on it), then from the repo folder: 

# one-time: install the serial library
python3 -m pip install pyserial

# run the tool
python3 firmware/splitflapfirmwarev7/splitflap_test.py \
    --port /dev/serial0 --baud 9600

A menu appears. Try, in order:

  • Home (module 0) — the drum should repeat its power-on dance.
  • Show character → A — the drum flips round and stops on A.
  • Spell something one letter at a time. You've built a split-flap display.

Under the hood each menu action sends a tiny text message down the A/B wires, like m0-A — "module 0, show character A". The chip does everything else.

If letters land slightly off

Two menu commands tune the module, and both save to the chip permanently:

  • Calibrate (c) — the module measures its own drum rotation and corrects step counts. Run this once.
  • Nudge (s) — if every character consistently stops, say, half a flap early, nudge forward a few steps at a time until "A" is perfectly framed. The correction is remembered.

If characters are off by whole flaps (you ask for A and always get C), the flaps were loaded into the drum shifted by that many positions — rehome, then reseat the flaps so the blank shows at the home position.

10 The web app

Same Pi, same wiring — when you're ready to graduate from the test tool: 

python3 -m pip install flask pyserial pytz yfinance requests

Then three small edits at the top of frontend/app.py:

  • Line ~14 — set the serial port to /dev/serial0.
  • Line ~16 — the settings path is hardcoded to /home/gordo/…; change it to your Pi username and mkdir -p ~/splitflap.
  • Bottom of the file — change port 80 to 5000 so it runs without sudo.
cd frontend
python3 app.py
# then browse to  http://<pi-address>:5000

The web app assumes the full 3 × 15 display and happily sends commands to all 45 module numbers; your lone module 0 simply answers the ones addressed to it — it sits at the top-left position, so it shows the first character of the top row of whatever page the app displays. Perfect for a one-character clock digit, less so for reading the weather. The app becomes properly useful once you build more modules — which, wiring-wise, is just this guide repeated with HARDCODED_ID = 1, 2, 3…

11 Troubleshooting

SymptomMost likely fixes, in order
Nothing at all on power-up Wall adapter actually on? Red/black in the right holes? Solder joints solid (tug test)? If you have a multimeter: ~12 V between the 12V and G holes.
Drum spins ~1½ turns, then stops somewhere random Homing failed — the Hall sensor never saw the magnet. Check the sensor plug is in, the sensor sits close to the drum's path, and if it still fails, the magnet is glued in upside-down: flip it.
Motor buzzes / twitches but won't turn Motor plug half-seated; or the motor is a 5 V 28BYJ-48 (the board needs the 12 V version); or the gear train is jammed — try turning the drum gently by hand with power off.
Powers up and homes fine, but the test tool gets no response Swap the blue and green wires at the converter's A+/B− (the classic RS-485 swap — completely safe). Check the TX/RX jumpers cross over (Pi TXD → module RXD). Confirm the serial console was disabled in raspi-config and the port is /dev/serial0 at 9600.
/dev/serial0 missing, or "permission denied" Re-run the raspi-config step and reboot. For permissions: sudo usermod -a -G dialout $USER, then log out and back in.
Upload fails in Arduino IDE Check Programmer is SerialUPDI — 230400 baud, the right port is chosen, the Friend's switch is on 3V, and the three wires match labels. Wiggle-test the connections and use Upload Using Programmer, not Upload.
Right letters, but drifts as it runs Run Calibrate from the test tool; check for debris/skipping in the printed gears.
